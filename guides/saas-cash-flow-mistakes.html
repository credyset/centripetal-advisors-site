Five cash-flow mistakes that distort SaaS runway.

How do you pressure-test a cash forecast before committing spend?

Start with one decision and its deadline. Trace the cash it depends on to dated receipts, committed payments, and reconciled opening balances. Then test the assumption most likely to move—and identify which commitment changes if it does.

A forecast can be mathematically correct and still carry an unsupported payment date or an unowned assumption. These five reviews help connect the model to the operating decision.

01 · Collections

A signed contract is carrying an unsupported receipt date.

The gap is often inside the process between signing and payment: implementation acceptance, a purchase-order requirement, invoice approval, or a customer’s payment cycle. A forecast can include the correct contract value and still place the receipt in the wrong week.

Review the receipt assumption.

For the receipts that matter to the decision, connect the contract or invoice to an expected payment date. Identify the evidence for that date: terms, billing milestones, recent payment behavior, and any unresolved approval or dispute. Mark unbilled work separately from issued invoices so those dependencies stay visible.

Future sales can appear as explicit assumptions in the longer-range plan. In the near-term view, distinguish them from documented receivables and expected billing. Test the commitments that depend on an uncertain receipt.

Evidence to bring: receivables aging, expected billing dates, customer payment history, and an owner for exceptions.

Decision to revisit: a start date or payment commitment that relies on a receipt before its timing is supported.

02 · Cash timing

The profit view is standing in for the cash calendar.

Revenue, invoices, and cash receipts answer different questions. An annual prepayment can support cash today while the related revenue is recognized over time. An expense can appear in the operating results in a different period from its payment. The SEC’s financial-statement guide explains the distinction between earnings and cash movement.

Keep the performance view connected to dated cash events.

Build the near-term cash view from expected receipts and actual payment schedules. Explain the bridge to the operating results, including receivables, payables, prepayments, and financing flows. Ask the accounting owner to confirm the company’s recognition policy for its actual contracts; there is no single recognition rule that fits every subscription, implementation, or usage arrangement.

Tag an annual customer payment by the period it covers and its next expected renewal. Keep financing proceeds and uncertain tax-credit receipts visible as distinct assumptions. A strong cash month should not automatically become the next month’s collection expectation.

Evidence to bring: a bridge between the P&L and cash movement, prepaid items, billing schedules, and financing assumptions.

Decision to revisit: recurring spending justified by cash whose next receipt follows a different timetable.

03 · Commitments

Last month’s burn is missing next month’s obligations.

Cash divided by historical net burn is a shorthand with a specific assumption: the future resembles the period used in the calculation. A changed hiring plan, annual renewal, debt payment, or delayed receipt can break that assumption.

Place each commitment in the week it affects cash.

Connect offer start dates to payroll runs and associated costs. Place vendor payments, annual renewals, taxes, and debt obligations on their expected dates. Separate payments already committed from choices the team can still change. A monthly average can conceal a tight week between receipts and payroll.

Then compare an explicit alternative: a delayed collection, a different hiring date, or changed financing timing. Choose assumptions based on the company’s situation, rather than applying a universal percentage change to every forecast.

Evidence to bring: payroll dates, offer commitments, payment schedules, debt obligations, and the operating model.

Decision to revisit: the earliest commitment that would change under the alternative assumption.

04 · Customer concentration

Customer concentration is visible in ARR, but absent from the cash scenario.

ARR concentration and near-term receipt concentration are related but different. A customer can represent a modest share of annual revenue and a large share of the cash expected in a particular week. Several large invoices can also depend on the same renewal or acceptance milestone.

Connect customer dependence to receipt timing.

Review the share of ARR held by the top customer, top three, and top five. Alongside it, identify the receipts that materially affect the cash path. Examine the relevant payment, renewal, and termination terms with the people who own those relationships.

Compare delayed payment separately from customer loss. A timing shift moves a receipt; a loss can change future receipts and delivery costs. Do not use one universal concentration cutoff as a verdict on the business. The exposure depends on the contracts, customer base, stage, and commitments behind the forecast.

Evidence to bring: customer-level revenue, expected receipts, renewal dates, and the assumptions used in each scenario.

Decision to revisit: whether a commitment still works when a material customer receipt moves.

05 · Close & ownership

The forecast has a refresh date, but no accountable refresh process.

A recent file timestamp does not show which balances were reconciled, which assumptions changed, or who approved them. An unexplained starting balance carries into every forecast week; a stale payment assumption survives until someone owns its correction.

Make the refresh traceable.

Reconcile the opening cash position to the company’s records and identify balances with restrictions on use. Explain outstanding timing differences. QuickBooks’ reconciliation guidance describes matching the statement and resolving the reconciliation difference, rather than treating a small unexplained difference as acceptable.

Compare actual receipts and payments with the prior forecast. Record what moved, why, the updated assumption, and its owner. Use a consistent chart of accounts and close process in QuickBooks or Xero, then connect the reporting owner to the people who control collections and commitments.

Evidence to bring: reconciliations, forecast-to-actual variances, an assumption log, and named owners.

Decision to revisit: any commitment supported by a balance or assumption that has not been reconciled or refreshed.

Cash access and approvals belong in the same conversation. Use the Treasury Hygiene guide to review who can access cash and authorize its movement.

    Questions to resolve with the finance team.

    Does a 13-week cash view replace the operating model?

    No. It supports near-term receipt and payment decisions. The longer-range operating model connects hiring, revenue, expenses, and capital assumptions beyond that horizon. Review the bridge between the two.

    How often should the forecast be refreshed?

    Use a weekly cadence for the rolling cash view, and revisit it when a material assumption or commitment changes. Compare actuals with the prior forecast before rolling the horizon forward. Xero’s forecasting guide covers payment timing, forecast horizons, and comparison with actual results.

    Does completing this worksheet tell us how much runway we have?

    No. It tracks the areas you have reviewed and the follow-ups you identify. It does not calculate runway, predict financing outcomes, or assign a readiness rating.

    What should we bring to a conversation about the forecast?

    Begin with the decision, deadline, and assumption that needs review. You do not need to upload financial documents through the concept contact page. The company records relevant to any subsequent work can be agreed separately.
