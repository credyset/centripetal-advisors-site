When the hiring plan and cash forecast tell different stories

A headline runway number can conceal delayed receipts, annual billing patterns, one-time commitments, or a hiring plan that has moved ahead of the model. A business can be tracking its budget while facing a different near-term cash position.

The useful question is which assumptions would change a decision. We connect cash movement to the operating plan, explain deviations from the prior forecast, and identify when a commitment or scenario needs to be revisited.

The work we help you own

Build the rolling cash view

Use a 13-week view to connect expected receipts with payroll, vendor payments, taxes, debt obligations, and other commitments. Tie opening balances to the company’s cash records.

Connect the longer-range plan

Link hiring dates, sales expectations, collections, and delivery costs to the operating forecast. Explain the differences between planned performance and actual cash timing.

Test decisions under different assumptions

Compare hiring sequences, collection delays, spending changes, and financing timing. Show which assumptions move the cash path and which commitments limit flexibility.

Maintain the review cadence

Refresh the forecast against actual receipts and payments. Explain timing and assumption variances, name the owner of each update, and record the decisions that follow.

How we begin

  1. Start with the hiring, spending, or financing decision that needs a clearer cash view.
  2. Reconcile balances and gather the receipt assumptions, payroll dates, payment commitments, and existing operating model.
  3. Agree the forecast owners, update cadence, and scenarios to use in the first review.

Questions founders ask

Why use a 13-week cash view alongside the budget?
The budget describes planned operating performance. The weekly cash view focuses on when money arrives and leaves. Connecting them helps explain how a change in collection timing or a payment commitment affects decisions without confusing it with a change in revenue performance.
What information is needed to begin?
Current balances, customer invoices and collection assumptions, payroll dates, committed payments, and the existing model are useful starting points. The first review identifies missing inputs and who will maintain them.
Do you refresh the forecast after building it?
A useful forecast needs an owner and a review cadence. Ongoing updates, variance explanations, and scenario reviews can be part of the engagement; the scope is agreed around the team and decisions.
Can the same model support a fundraising or debt discussion?
Yes. The operating assumptions should connect across the cash forecast, capital plan, and financing materials. We adapt the supporting schedules and scenarios to the questions being considered.
