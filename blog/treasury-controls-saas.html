Treasury Controls Every SaaS Founder Should Have Before the Next Board Meeting

Treasury controls are not a post-Series B concern. A 30-minute review of bank structure, FDIC coverage, signatory controls, and sweep mechanics can prevent avoidable risk.

Why treasury is not a later-stage problem

Most early-stage SaaS founders do not think about treasury until something forces them to: a bank failure makes the news, an auditor asks about FDIC coverage, or a board member asks who has signatory authority.

The truth is that treasury controls are straightforward to set up and easy to neglect. A 30-minute review can surface risks that would otherwise go unnoticed until they become expensive.

The five controls that matter

Bank structure: are operating funds and reserve funds separated? If all cash sits in one account, a single fraud event or bank issue affects everything.

FDIC coverage: does the company's cash exceed the $250,000 per-depositor insurance limit? If so, is there a sweep arrangement or a second banking relationship?

Signatory controls: who can authorize payments, wire transfers, and account changes? If one person can move all the company's cash without a second approval, that is a control gap.

Sweep mechanics: is excess cash earning yield in a money market or treasury fund, or is it sitting in a zero-interest checking account?

Fraud prevention: are ACH and wire controls in place? Is positive pay enabled? Are payment thresholds set for dual approval?

How to do the review

Take 30 minutes. Pull up each bank account. Check the balance against the FDIC limit. Verify the authorized signers. Check whether sweep or yield is enabled. Confirm the fraud prevention controls.

Document the answers. If anything is missing, fix it. This is not a quarterly project — it is a one-time setup with an annual check.

When the board asks

The next time a board member asks about treasury controls, the right answer is: here is our bank structure, here is our FDIC coverage, here are the authorized signers, here are the fraud controls, and here is when we last reviewed them.

That answer takes 30 minutes to prepare. Not having it takes longer to explain.

Frequently Asked Questions

What treasury controls should an early-stage SaaS company have?
Separated operating and reserve accounts, FDIC coverage awareness with sweep or multi-bank arrangements if cash exceeds $250K, dual-signatory controls, fraud prevention (positive pay, ACH blocks, wire thresholds), and a documented review date.
How long does a treasury review take?
About 30 minutes for the initial review: check each bank account balance against FDIC limits, verify authorized signers, confirm sweep and fraud controls, and document the results. Annual re-checks take less.
When should a startup worry about FDIC coverage?
As soon as any single bank account holds more than $250,000. This often happens shortly after a fundraise, when the full amount is deposited into one operating account.

Put this into practice

Make the topic part of the operating cadence, not a one-time cleanup project. Assign one owner, identify the source system for every number, and decide how often the underlying schedule will be refreshed. The goal is a repeatable answer that does not change depending on who prepares the deck or which spreadsheet is open.

At each review, reconcile the operating view to the accounting view and document any difference. A useful review note states what changed, why it changed, whether the variance is timing or performance, and what decision follows. That discipline is what makes a metric or process usable in a board meeting, financing conversation, or diligence request.

Questions to resolve before the next review

  • Who owns the number or process, and what system is the source of truth?
  • What definition is used consistently across the model, reporting package, and board deck?
  • Which variance would trigger a decision rather than another round of analysis?
  • What supporting schedule should a lender or investor be able to inspect?
