Three roles, three jobs

A bookkeeper processes transactions, reconciles accounts, and produces the raw financial records. A controller owns the close process, ensures the books are accurate, manages compliance, and produces reliable monthly financials. A CFO interprets the financials, makes capital decisions, builds the financial story for investors and boards, and connects financial planning to operating strategy.

These are not seniority levels. They are different jobs.

What most early-stage companies get wrong

The most common mistake is hiring a CFO when the company needs a controller. If the monthly close takes two weeks, if the chart of accounts is a mess, if revenue recognition is inconsistent, those are controller problems. A CFO will not fix them — or will fix them at 3x the cost.

The second most common mistake is expecting a bookkeeper to do controller work. A bookkeeper who also does payroll, benefits administration, and ad-hoc reporting is stretched. The work may get done, but accuracy and timeliness suffer.

How to map the work to the role

List the finance work the company actually does in a given month. Categorize each task: is it transaction processing, close and compliance, or strategy and judgment?

If 80% of the work is transaction processing, the company needs a bookkeeper. If the close and compliance layer is growing, it needs a controller. If the founder is spending meaningful time on capital decisions, board preparation, or investor conversations, it needs CFO-level judgment — which can come from a fractional CFO before a full-time hire is justified.

The fractional layer

A fractional CFO sits between the controller and a full-time CFO hire. It is the right choice when the company needs senior financial judgment but does not yet need — or cannot yet afford — a full-time finance executive.

The fractional model works when the highest-value work is strategic, not operational. When the operational finance workload grows to need a daily owner, that is when the VP Finance conversation begins.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should a startup hire a controller?
When the monthly close is unreliable, revenue recognition is inconsistent, the chart of accounts needs restructuring, or the bookkeeper is stretched beyond transaction processing into compliance and reporting work.
What is the difference between a controller and a CFO?
A controller owns accuracy: close process, compliance, and reliable financials. A CFO owns interpretation: capital decisions, board reporting, investor narrative, and connecting financial planning to operating strategy.
Can a bookkeeper handle early-stage SaaS finance?
A bookkeeper can handle transaction processing and basic reconciliation. Once the company needs reliable monthly close, revenue recognition policy, or compliance reporting, the work exceeds the bookkeeper role.
