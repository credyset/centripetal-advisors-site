Make the forecast explainable

A useful forecast connects actual results, explicit assumptions, and the operating decisions that follow. Accuracy remains a review concern, but a single output also needs a clear explanation of what would change under a different assumption. Investor expectations vary; the model does not establish trust or approval by itself.

Show the bridge from actual to planned

Document the last actual period, the forecast cutoff, and the sources of the starting balances. Explain why planned growth, hiring, or collections differ from recent results. Distinguish evidence-supported assumptions from management judgments and unresolved estimates.

Separate new revenue, expansion, contraction, and churn where relevant. Connect hiring dates and payment commitments to the cash view. Keep a record of who owns each material assumption and when it was last reviewed.

Choose a scenario that tests a commitment

Change an assumption relevant to the company rather than adding a generic downside percentage. Explain which operating commitment changes first, who can respond, and what evidence would reopen the decision.

Illustrative example: an implementation delay moves billing. Follow the consequence through the receipt schedule and planned spending. The review should reveal both the timing dependency and the responsible owners.

Compare actual performance with the prior forecast and record what changed. Use the cash guide below to review the evidence supporting the model, rather than treating a scenario count as a readiness standard.
