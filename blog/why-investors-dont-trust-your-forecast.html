Why Investors Don't Trust Your Forecast

Can a reader follow an assumption change through the forecast to a decision?

The trust problem is not accuracy

Investors do not expect the forecast to be right. They expect it to be decision-ready.

A decision-ready forecast shows what changes if a hire slips, if collections slow, if a large customer churns, or if growth comes in below plan. It connects revenue assumptions to hiring assumptions to cash assumptions to capital assumptions. When one input changes, the downstream effects are visible.

A forecast that only works in the base case is not a planning instrument. It is a wish.

Three things that break trust

First: the forecast does not tie to historical performance. If the model projects 3x growth but the last two quarters were flat, the gap between history and projection needs an explicit explanation.

Second: the forecast does not show what happens when something goes wrong. Investors will test the downside. If the model has no downside case, the investor assumes the founder has not thought about it.

Third: the assumptions are hidden. A model that shows output without making the inputs visible — hiring timeline, quota ramp, deal size assumptions, churn rate — looks like a black box.

What a credible forecast includes

Base case, upside case, and downside case — each tied to specific assumptions that can be tested. A hiring plan with costs and ramp timelines. Revenue by source (new logo, expansion, renewal) rather than a single top-line number. Cash implications of each scenario, including when the company would need to raise again or adjust spending.

The forecast should be a conversation starter, not a promise.

The Centripetal lens

A credible forecast does not predict the future. It describes the decision framework the management team is using to navigate uncertainty. Investors invest in the framework, not the prediction.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do investors distrust startup forecasts?
Usually because the forecast only works in the base case, does not tie to historical performance, hides its assumptions, or does not show what management would do if the downside case became reality.
What makes a SaaS forecast credible?
Three scenarios tied to testable assumptions, a hiring plan with costs, revenue disaggregated by source, cash implications per scenario, and a clear description of the decision framework behind the numbers.
Should a startup forecast be accurate?
Investors do not expect accuracy. They expect decision-readiness: a model that shows what changes when inputs change, and a management team that knows which levers to pull.
