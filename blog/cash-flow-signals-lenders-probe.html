The Cash-Flow Signals Lenders Probe That a 13-Week Model Can Hide

A 13-week cash-flow model is only the beginning. Here are the committed-spend, collection, covenant, and scenario signals lenders use to read the downside.

A 13-week cash-flow model can be accurate and still leave a lender with the wrong impression.

The reason is simple: a weekly cash view tells you when cash moves. It does not always explain which commitments are reversible, which collections are fragile, or what happens when a covenant is tested.

This is the lender's lens looking into your books.

Start with committed versus discretionary spend

Separate payroll, rent, debt service, contracted vendors, and other obligations you cannot quickly remove from discretionary hiring, projects, and vendors you can pause. Two companies with the same weekly burn can have very different room to maneuver.

A lender wants to know what happens if collections arrive late or a planned hire moves forward. If every expense is treated as equally flexible, the model hides the real downside.

Make collection lag visible

Cash flow should show the difference between invoiced revenue and collected cash. Review AR aging, payment terms, renewal timing, and the customers that account for a meaningful share of open receivables.

A model that assumes every invoice converts on schedule is not conservative. It is incomplete.

For SaaS companies with annual contracts, the question is not only how much ARR exists. It is when the cash arrives, what sits behind it, and what changes if one large customer pays late.

Map covenant and runway triggers

Do not wait until a lender sends a covenant schedule. Put the triggers in the operating model: minimum cash, reporting dates, revenue or performance tests, borrowing-base mechanics where relevant, and the actions management would take if a threshold is approached.

The useful question is not “How many months of runway do we have?” It is “What decision changes first if the downside case becomes the base case?”

Run three views, not one

Use a base case, a delayed-collections case, and a slower-growth case. Tie each to specific actions: pause a hiring cohort, renegotiate a vendor, reduce discretionary spend, or revisit the capital plan.

The model becomes lender-ready when it shows judgment, not just arithmetic.

The Centripetal lens

A 13-week model is a useful instrument. It is not the whole cash story. Lenders are looking for the structure behind the number: what is committed, what is collectible, what is covenant-sensitive, and what management will do next.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a 13-week cash-flow model enough for venture debt diligence?
No. It should be paired with committed-spend analysis, AR collection assumptions, covenant triggers, existing obligations, and downside scenarios tied to management actions.
What cash-flow detail do lenders often question first?
The gap between invoiced revenue and collected cash, especially when a small number of customers represent a meaningful share of open receivables.
How should a SaaS company prepare for a covenant discussion?
Put minimum cash, reporting dates, performance tests, and response actions directly into the operating model before the lender asks for them.
